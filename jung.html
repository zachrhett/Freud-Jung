Founder of analytical psychology

Carl Gustav Jung

Swiss psychiatrist. From the word-association experiment to the collective unconscious, individuation, and a psychology of symbols.

Life in outline

1875 Born in Kesswil, Thurgau, son of a country pastor.
1900–09 Burghölzli clinic under Eugen Bleuler. Word-association research; theory of complexes.
1907 First meeting with Freud in Vienna. Hours of conversation.
1910–13 First president of the International Psychoanalytic Association. Break with Freud after Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido.
1913–18 Confrontation with the unconscious. The Black Books; later the Red Book (Liber Novus).
1921 Psychological Types.
1930s–50s Alchemy, synchronicity, flying saucers, the Self; Terry Lectures on psychology and religion.
1961 Dies at Küsnacht, 6 June.

The system

Jung accepted the reality of the unconscious and the value of the analytic relationship, then rebuilt the map. Libido becomes general psychic energy, not only sexual. The personal unconscious is only the upper layer. Beneath it lies a collective unconscious whose contents are archetypes—typical forms of experience that appear as images, motifs, and patterns of relationship.

Complexes

From the association experiment: a complex is an affect-toned cluster of ideas, often autonomous, that can seize speech, memory, and mood. “We do not have complexes; complexes can have us.”

Archetypes (a short list, not a catalog)

  • Persona — the social mask.
  • Shadow — disowned personal qualities, often first met in projection.
  • Anima / Animus — contrasexual inner figures; mediators to the unconscious.
  • Self — the organizing center and totality of the psyche, symbolized by mandala, quaternity, divine child, stone.
  • Others frequently discussed — the wise old man/woman, the trickster, the hero, the mother, rebirth.

An archetype is not a fixed picture in a museum. It is a form that fills with the material of a life and a culture.

Individuation

The lifelong process of becoming an undivided person: withdrawing projections, relating to the unconscious, and relating the ego to the Self. It is not self-improvement and not a cult of personality. Jung treated it as a natural process that analysis can accompany, especially at midlife.

Psychological types

Two attitudes: extraversion and introversion. Four functions: thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition. One function is usually superior, one inferior. Later questionnaires (including MBTI) popularized a simplified grid; they are not Jung’s book and they are separately copyrighted.

Method

Dreams are compensatory, not only wish-fulfilling. Interpretation uses amplification (personal associations plus myth, religion, alchemy, folk tale) rather than a fixed symbol dictionary. Active imagination lets unconscious figures speak in waking fantasy, writing, or painting—always with the ego present. Transference is real, but so is the larger symbolic field.
