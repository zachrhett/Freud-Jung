Published histories

Classic case studies

Original summaries of the cases that built the field. Full published texts belong to their editions; several early reports are public domain.

These are teaching summaries of historically published material. They omit clinical detail that would identify living people and do not reproduce copyrighted translations at length.

Anna O. (Bertha Pappenheim) — Breuer, 1880–82

Reported in Studies on Hysteria (1895). A young woman with a constellation of conversion symptoms, dissociation, and a “talking cure” that she herself named. Breuer used hypnosis and daily narrative of symptoms. The case became the founding myth of psychoanalysis—and later a field of historical correction (the ending was less tidy than the published story). Pappenheim became a major social reformer and feminist.

Why it matters: catharsis, the talking cure, the limits of hypnosis, the ethics of writing a patient.

Dora (Ida Bauer) — Freud, 1901 / published 1905

An adolescent girl brought by her father. Freud reads her symptoms through a web of family sexual intrigue and interrupted treatment. The essay is as famous for what it shows about Freud’s countertransference and narrative force as for its theory of hysteria and dreams.

Why it matters: dream interpretation in a case, the problem of the analyst’s desire, feminist and historical rereadings.

Little Hans (Herbert Graf) — Freud, 1909

A five-year-old with a horse phobia, analyzed largely through the father’s reports. Freud presents it as confirmation of infantile sexuality and the Oedipus complex. Later commentators have asked how much the theory organized the evidence.

Why it matters: child analysis before child analysis existed; the status of reconstruction.

The Rat Man (Ernst Lanzer) — Freud, 1909

An obsessional neurosis organized around a torture fantasy, debt, and conflicting loves. The case is the classic demonstration of ambivalence, magical thinking, and the rat as condensed symbol.

Why it matters: technique with obsessional structure; how a single image carries a history.

The Wolf Man (Sergei Pankejeff) — Freud, 1918

An infantile neurosis reconstructed from an adult analysis, including the famous dream of wolves in a tree. The case became a decades-long relationship and, later, a contest of memoirs. It is the high-water mark of reconstruction of a primal scene.

Why it matters: how far reconstruction can go; the afterlife of a patient who outlives his analyst’s narrative.

Schreber — Freud, 1911

Not a patient of Freud’s. Daniel Paul Schreber’s Memoirs of My Nervous Illness (1903) is read as a paranoid system: God, rays, unmanning, the need to redeem the world. Freud interprets the delusion as a defense against a homosexual wish toward the father. Later readers (Lacan, Schatzman, Santner) reread power, family pedagogy, and language.

Why it matters: psychoanalysis meeting psychosis on the ground of a text.

Jung at the Burghölzli

Jung’s early papers on dementia praecox (schizophrenia) and the association experiment treat psychotic language as meaningful—an important bridge and an early difference from a purely deficit model. The “Miller Fantasies” analyzed in Psychology of the Unconscious (1912/1916) are not a therapy case in the usual sense: Jung interprets published fantasies of an American woman he did not treat, and in doing so works out his break with Freud. That book is in the U.S. public domain.

How to study a case

  1. Read the published narrative once for the story.
  2. List the symptoms and the interpretations offered.
  3. Ask what evidence would falsify the interpretation.
  4. Note the analyst’s rhetoric—certainty, analogy, omission.
  5. Read one later commentary that disagrees.
